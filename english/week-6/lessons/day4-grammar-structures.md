## Pattern 1 — Embedded Question as a Statement

### Transcript Quote
> *"What is trust? And who should we place our trust in? — these are some of the questions we'll be discussing in this programme."*

### Communicative Function
Used to **introduce a topic** by listing its key questions, making the audience feel curious. Common in presentations, reports, and podcast openings.

### Template
> **[Question 1]? And [Question 2]? — these are some of the issues we'll be exploring.**

### Examples
- *What is data quality? And how do we measure it? — these are some of the challenges every data engineer faces.*
- *Who should have access to player data? And how should it be protected? — these are questions that matter in every casino operation.*

### Your Turn
> Think of a problem or topic from your work. Frame it using this pattern:
> *"What is ___? And how do we ___? — these are some of the challenges we'll be discussing."*

## Pattern 2 — Defining a Concept with a Relative Clause

### Transcript Quote
> *"We'll be hearing from a philosopher who believes the problem is not about trust itself but about trustworthiness."*

### Communicative Function
Used to **introduce an expert or idea clearly** using a defining relative clause ("who believes…"). Common in professional reports and presentations.

### Template
> **[Subject] + who/which + [defining belief or action] + [core argument].**

### Examples
- *We'll be reviewing a system which processes player data in real time but lacks sufficient audit logging.*
- *I spoke with a stakeholder who believed the pipeline was stable, but in fact it had a critical reliability issue.*

### Your Turn
> Introduce someone from your work context:
> *"I worked with a [colleague / manager / vendor] who believed ___, but in fact ___."*

## Pattern 3 — Contrast with "not … but …"

### Transcript Quote
> *"The problem is not about trust itself but about trustworthiness."*

### Communicative Function
Used to **correct a common misconception** or sharpen a point by redirecting attention. Highly effective in professional communication and technical explanation.

### Template
> **The issue is not [misconception] but [actual problem].**

### Examples
- *The problem is not the volume of data but the quality of the source system.*
- *The error was not in the SQL logic but in the data type mismatch at ingestion.*
- *It's not about working harder but about instrumenting the right metrics.*

### Your Turn
> Correct a common misunderstanding from your field:
> *"The problem is not ___ but ___."*

## Pattern 4 — Conditional Warning with "if you … then …"

### Transcript Quote
> *"If you simply place trust indiscriminately without making a judgement about whether the other person or institution is trustworthy, then just trusting to luck… is probably not a virtue."*

### Communicative Function
Used to **warn against a risky or naïve approach** by linking action to consequence. The formal structure signals a logical, reasoned argument.

### Template
> **If you [risky action] without [proper check], then [consequence].**

### Examples
- *If you ingest raw files without validating schema changes, then you risk corrupting downstream models.*
- *If you grant access indiscriminately without auditing permissions, then your data warehouse becomes a security risk.*

### Your Turn
> Write a warning relevant to your work:
> *"If you [action] without [check], then [consequence]."*

## Pattern 5 — Three-Part List with Escalating Importance

### Transcript Quote
> *"To be trustworthy they need three ingredients. First, honesty… Second, competence. Beyond honesty and competence there's a third element to trustworthiness: reliability."*

### Communicative Function
Used to **present a structured argument** in an ordered, memorable way. "Beyond X and Y there is Z" gives extra weight to the final element. Excellent for presentations and professional explanations.

### Template
> **First, [element 1]. Second, [element 2]. Beyond [1] and [2] there is a third element: [element 3].**

### Examples
- *First, the pipeline needs to be accurate. Second, it needs to be fast. Beyond accuracy and speed, there is a third requirement: reliability at scale.*
- *First, the dashboard must display correct data. Second, it must load quickly. Beyond those two requirements, there is a third criterion: it must be maintainable by the business team.*

### Your Turn
> Apply this structure to a technical or professional concept you know well.

## Pattern 6 — Dismissive Reframe ("That's the boring one.")

### Transcript Quote
> *"That's the boring one. That's just being honest and competent each time so that it's not enough to be episodically honest and competent for some of the things you claim to be able to do but not others."*

### Communicative Function
Used to **downplay an element before redefining its importance**. The self-deprecating tone ("boring") makes the speaker sound authentic while still making a sharp point. Very effective in informal presentations and interviews.

### Template
> **That's the [adjective] one. That's just [simple restatement], meaning [deeper implication].**

### Examples
- *That's the unglamorous part. That's just making sure every DAG runs on schedule — but without it, none of the downstream reports exist.*
- *That sounds simple. That's just logging every API call — but it's what makes debugging possible six months later.*

### Your Turn
> Pick a "boring but essential" task from your work. Reframe it using this pattern.

## Pattern 7 — Negative Conditional to Define Scope ("you wouldn't … but you would …")

### Transcript Quote
> *"You would trust a car mechanic to fix your broken car engine, but you wouldn't go to them for dental work — they're not competent to remove your tooth like a dentist is."*

### Communicative Function
Used to **illustrate the limits of competence through contrast**. This is a classic example-based explanation technique — highly effective in teaching and professional presentations to non-technical audiences.

### Template
> **You would trust a [role] to [task A], but you wouldn't ask them to [task B] — they're not competent to [task B] like a [other role] is.**

### Examples
- *You would trust a data engineer to build the pipeline, but you wouldn't ask them to design the marketing strategy — they're not competent in consumer psychology like a CMO is.*
- *You would trust me to optimise the SQL query, but you wouldn't ask me to configure the network firewall — that's outside my domain of competence.*

### Your Turn
> Describe the limits of your own professional competence honestly using this pattern.
