## 📘 Rule 1: Passive Voice with Modals: `will be held` vs. `take place`

* **Quote**: *"The formal architecture review **will be held on Zoom** this Thursday at 2 PM."*
* **Rule Explanation**: 
  * `HOLD` is a transitive verb meaning *to organize / host*. An event, meeting, or incident post-mortem cannot organize itself; it must be hosted by people. Therefore, with future modals, use the passive:
    $$\text{[Event]} + \mathbf{will\ be\ held} + \text{in [City/Country] / at [Venue] / on [Platform/Day]}$$
  * Conversely, `take place` is inherently intransitive (*to happen / occur*). It **never takes the passive voice**:
    $$\text{[Event]} + \mathbf{will\ take\ place} + \text{in [City] / on [Day]}$$
* **Common Pitfall**:
  > ❌ **Incorrect**: *The architecture review will hold on Zoom.* (Missing passive auxiliary).  
  > ❌ **Incorrect**: *The disaster recovery drill will be taken place on Saturday.* (Incorrect passive).  
  > ✅ **Correct**: *The review will **be held on Zoom**.* / *The drill will **take place on Saturday**.*

## 📘 Rule 2: Conversational Ellipsis with Substitute Auxiliary: `often do` vs. `often are`

* **Quote**: *"The caching layer was acting up yesterday afternoon, **as those things often do** during heavy migrations."*
* **Rule Explanation**: 
  In spoken English, auxiliary verbs (`do/does` or `be`) act as pro-forms replacing entire predicate phrases. This creates crisp, native commentary without clumsy repetition:
  * If the preceding verb is an **Action Verb** (*fail, crash, time out, act up*): use **`often do / does`**.
  * If the preceding verb is **To Be / An Adjective** (*slow, fragile, expensive*): use **`often are / is`**.
* **Common Pitfall**:
  > ❌ **Incorrect Auxiliary**: *Are spot instances unreliable? — Yeah, cheap nodes often do.* (Question uses adjective `unreliable`; response must use `often are`).  
  > ✅ **Natural Native English**: *Do legacy scripts time out? — Yeah, unindexed ones **often do**.* / *Are cloud databases expensive? — Managed ones **often are**.*

## 📘 Rule 3: Comparative Precision & Concession with `as ... as` & `as much as`

* **Quote**: *"**As much as** we want low query latency, we cannot afford to provision dedicated compute for cold data."*
* **Rule Explanation**: 
  * **Capacity / Extent Comparison**: `not as [Adj] as [Standard]` or `not available as much as [Requirement]` sets realistic limits.
  * **Concession / Polite Balancing**: `As much as + [Clause], [Main Clause]` concedes a valid desire while asserting an unavoidable constraint (*dù rất muốn... nhưng...*).
* **Common Pitfall**:
  > ❌ **Overly Formal / Clunky**: *Although we desire fast queries very much, however, the price is high.*  
  > ✅ **Natural Native English**: * **As much as we'd like** instant queries, paying for provisioned IOPS is just **throwing money down the drain**.*
